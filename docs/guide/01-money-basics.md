# 1. Money basics for a salaried person

There is no software in this chapter. This is the part that actually changes your finances — the app in the following chapters is just a way to keep score.

If you already understand your payslip, know what an emergency fund is, and have a target savings rate, skip to [chapter 2](02-first-hour.md).

---

## 1.1 Your salary is four different numbers, and only one of them is yours

When people say "I earn 36 lakhs," they usually mean CTC, which is the least useful of the four. Here is Aarav's actual payslip, and what each line means.

**Cost to Company (CTC): ₹36.6 lakh/year.** What your employer spends on you in total. It includes money you will never see as cash, like the employer's PF contribution and a gratuity provision. Recruiters quote this because it is the biggest number. Ignore it for planning.

**Gross salary: ₹2,85,000/month.** What your employment contract says before anything is deducted.

| Component | Monthly | What it is |
|-----------|---------|------------|
| Basic salary | ₹1,20,000 | The core figure. PF, gratuity and HRA exemption are all calculated from it |
| House Rent Allowance (HRA) | ₹60,000 | Tax-exempt only if you actually pay rent. Aarav lives with his parents, so he pays full tax on this |
| Special allowance | ₹1,05,000 | The leftover. Fully taxable |
| **Gross** | **₹2,85,000** | |

**Deductions: ₹65,000/month.**

| Deduction | Monthly | What it is |
|-----------|---------|------------|
| Employee PF (EPF) | ₹14,400 | 12% of basic. This is *your money*, going into your retirement account. It is not a tax |
| Professional tax | ₹200 | A small state-level tax. Karnataka charges ₹200/month |
| TDS (income tax) | ₹50,400 | Tax Deducted at Source — your employer pays your income tax on your behalf, monthly |
| **Total** | **₹65,000** | |

**In-hand (net) salary: ₹2,20,000/month.** What lands in your bank account. This is the only number that matters for budgeting, and it is the number to use everywhere in this guide.

> **The tax bit, briefly.** India has two tax regimes and the slabs change with almost every Union Budget, so any specific numbers printed here would be stale within a year. What matters for planning: your employer estimates your annual tax and deducts one-twelfth of it each month. If you have deductions to claim (rent, PPF, insurance premiums under the old regime), **declare them to your employer early in the financial year**, otherwise TDS is over-deducted and you wait until you file returns to get it back. Check the current year's slabs on the income tax department's site rather than trusting a blog.

### The number nobody tells you

Aarav's EPF gets ₹14,400 from him **and ₹14,400 from his employer** every month. That is **₹28,800/month, ₹3.46 lakh a year**, already being invested for his retirement before he does anything at all.

This matters psychologically. Most people feel they save nothing. Many are already saving 10–12% of gross without noticing. Count it. It goes in your net worth in [chapter 8](08-recurring-and-investments.md).

## 1.2 Where the money actually goes

Every rupee of your in-hand salary goes into one of three buckets. That is the entire theory of personal finance.

**Needs** — things that cause real problems if unpaid. Rent, groceries, electricity, transport to work, insurance premiums, minimum loan payments, medicines.

**Wants** — everything you would still be fine without. Eating out, subscriptions, holidays, gadgets, the nicer brand of anything. Wants are not a moral failing. Spending on them is the point of earning money. They just need a ceiling.

**Savings and investments** — money you deliberately do not spend. Emergency fund, mutual funds, PPF, extra loan repayment.

The distinction between needs and wants is honest, not flattering. Your phone bill is a need; the ₹1,499 plan when the ₹599 one works is partly a want. Broadband is a need if you work from home. A gym is a want that you should probably keep. Do not spend a week agonising over the boundary — a rough split you actually track beats a perfect split you abandon.

### 50/30/20, and why it is probably wrong for you

The famous rule says: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings. It is a fine default for someone paying market rent on a modest salary. It is a **terrible** default for two common situations:

- **You live with family and pay no rent.** Your needs are artificially low, and this is temporary. Saving only 20% here is wasting the best saving years you will ever have.
- **You earn a lot.** Needs do not scale with income. Someone on ₹2 lakh a month does not need ₹1 lakh of necessities.

Aarav's split, given no rent and no loans:

| Bucket | Amount | Share |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Needs | ₹52,000 | 24% |
| Wants | ₹38,000 | 17% |
| Save and invest | ₹1,30,000 | **59%** |

His needs in detail: ₹25,000 contributed to the household, ₹8,000 food at work, ₹7,500 miscellaneous essentials, ₹6,000 transport, ₹2,500 health insurance top-up, ₹1,500 term insurance, ₹1,500 phone and internet.

His wants: ₹12,000 eating out and delivery, ₹12,000 shopping and gadgets, ₹10,000 set aside for travel, ₹4,000 subscriptions and gym.

**The honest framing:** living with your parents at 27 is a temporary financial superpower worth roughly ₹40,000 a month in Bengaluru. Aarav will move out, or marry, or both, and his needs will double. The right response is to convert that advantage into assets now, not to inflate his lifestyle to fill the space.

**Your target:** whatever your situation, work out your savings rate — *(in-hand − total spending) ÷ in-hand* — and try to move it up by a few percentage points a year. The absolute number matters less than the direction. Securo shows you this figure as **`Savings Rate`** on the dashboard.

## 1.3 The emergency fund comes first

Before any investing. Before any mutual fund. Before you read a single thing about the stock market.

An emergency fund is cash you can reach within a day, set aside for the specific situations where you would otherwise borrow: losing your job, a medical bill your insurance argues about, a parent needing help, an urgent flight home.

**How much:** six months of your *total monthly spending* — needs plus wants, because in a real emergency you will not instantly cut your life to the bone.

For Aarav: (₹52,000 + ₹38,000) × 6 = **₹5,40,000**.

**Where to keep it:** a plain savings account, or a liquid fund, or a sweep-in fixed deposit. The goal is *available tomorrow morning*, not *high returns*. You will be told that keeping ₹5 lakh in a savings account is stupid because it loses to inflation. Ignore this. The emergency fund is insurance, not an investment, and the premium you pay is a couple of percent of return on a small slice of your money.

**Where not to keep it:** equity mutual funds, stocks, anything with a lock-in, and — critically — **not as an unused credit card limit**. A credit card is not an emergency fund. It is a loan at 36–42% annual interest, which converts a bad month into a bad year.

**Do not have one yet?** Build it first, fast, before starting SIPs. Aarav puts ₹45,000 a month into it and is done in twelve months. If that feels slow, remember he is investing ₹85,000 a month alongside it — he can afford to do both. If you cannot, do the emergency fund alone until it is finished.

You will track this as a **Goal** in Securo — see [chapter 7](07-budgets-and-goals.md).

## 1.4 Insurance before investment

Two policies, and they are not investments. Buy them for protection and nothing else.

**Term life insurance.** Pays out a lump sum if you die. Cover of roughly 10–15× your annual income. For a healthy 27-year-old this costs surprisingly little — Aarav pays ₹1,500 a month for ₹2 crore of cover.

If nobody depends on your income financially, you may not need this yet. If your parents, spouse or children would struggle without your salary, you do.

**Health insurance.** Your employer's group policy disappears the day you resign or are laid off — which is also, statistically, a stressful period. Buy a personal policy on top of it, and buy it while you are young and healthy, because pre-existing conditions get excluded later.

**What to avoid:** anything marketed as insurance *and* investment — endowment plans, money-back policies, ULIPs. They do both jobs badly, carry high commissions, and are hard to exit. Keep insurance and investing separate. If a relative who sells insurance is reading over your shoulder, this is still true.

## 1.5 The order of operations

When you have spare money, this is the sequence. Finish each step before moving to the next.

1. **Pay every credit card bill in full, every month.** At 36–42% a year, credit card debt beats every investment return available to you. Nothing else competes.
2. **Get health and term insurance in place.**
3. **Build the six-month emergency fund.**
4. **Clear any other high-interest debt** — personal loans, consumer EMIs above about 10–12%.
5. **Use tax-advantaged accounts** — your EPF is already running. PPF adds ₹1.5 lakh a year with a long lock-in and tax-free returns. NPS is worth considering for the additional deduction if you are comfortable with retirement-age lock-in.
6. **Invest the rest in low-cost diversified equity**, typically an index fund via monthly SIP, held for years.
7. **Only then** consider individual stocks, crypto, or anything a colleague is excited about — and with money you can afford to lose entirely.

Aarav's ₹1,30,000 monthly allocation in year one:

| Where | Amount | Why |
|-------|--------|-----|
| Emergency fund | ₹45,000 | Step 3. Stops after month 12, then redirects to SIP |
| Index fund SIP | ₹45,000 | Step 6 |
| PPF | ₹12,500 | Step 5 — ₹1.5 lakh/year |
| NPS | ₹10,000 | Step 5 |
| Gold or debt fund | ₹10,000 | Diversification |
| Unallocated | ₹7,500 | Deliberate slack, see below |

### Leave slack

The ₹7,500 with no name is the most important line in that table. Budgets that allocate every rupee fail on the first unexpected expense, and the failure feels like a personal one rather than an arithmetic one. Leave 5–10% unassigned. Real life happens in that gap.

## 1.6 Sinking funds: the trick that stops "unexpected" expenses

Most expenses people call unexpected are not. Diwali arrives every year. Insurance premiums renew annually. Your phone will die within four years. Weddings in the family cluster in season.

A **sinking fund** is money set aside monthly for a known, irregular, future expense.

Aarav's ₹10,000/month travel line is one: by December he has ₹1.2 lakh and takes a holiday without touching savings or reaching for a credit card. If instead he booked a ₹1.2 lakh trip on his card in December, that single month would look catastrophic in his reports and he might carry a balance into January.

Worth setting up sinking funds for: annual insurance premiums, festival spending and gifts, travel, phone and laptop replacement, vehicle servicing and insurance, medical costs your insurance doesn't cover.

In Securo you can model these either as monthly **budgets** in a category you spend from occasionally, or as **goals** you contribute to. [Chapter 7](07-budgets-and-goals.md) covers both, and when each is the better fit.

## 1.7 Credit cards, without the panic

A credit card used correctly is genuinely useful: it gives you 20–50 interest-free days, cashback or points, fraud protection better than a debit card, and it builds a credit history you will want when you apply for a home loan.

A credit card used incorrectly is the most expensive loan a normal person can get.

**The only rule that matters: pay the full statement balance, every month, automatically.**

Not the "minimum amount due." Paying the minimum is how the whole business model works. When you carry a balance, interest is typically charged from the *transaction date* on your entire balance, and often your new purchases lose their interest-free period too. A ₹50,000 balance at 3.5% a month, paying the ₹2,500 minimum, takes years and costs more than the original purchase.

**No-cost EMI** is worth understanding: the interest usually still exists, it is just paid as an upfront discount by the merchant, or bundled into the price. It is not a scam, but it is not free either, and it commits your future months. Securo has an **installments** feature that spreads an EMI purchase across future months so you can see the commitment — [chapter 4](04-transactions.md).

One habit worth building: check your credit card statement line by line once a month. It is the single most likely place for a fraudulent or forgotten recurring charge to hide. Importing it into Securo makes this pleasant rather than tedious.

## 1.8 The two numbers to watch

Ignore the rest until these two are moving in the right direction.

**Savings rate** — the share of your take-home pay you do not spend. It is the fastest lever you control. Going from a 20% to a 30% savings rate does more for your future than any fund selection will. Securo shows it as **`Savings Rate`** on the dashboard.

**Net worth** — everything you own minus everything you owe. It goes up and down; the trend over years is what counts. It is the only number that captures your whole financial picture in one figure. Securo shows it under **Reports → `Net Worth`**.

Check them monthly. Not daily — daily checking teaches you nothing and makes market noise feel like news.

## What to do with this chapter

Before opening the app, write down four things on paper:

1. Your actual monthly in-hand salary.
2. Your emergency fund target (six months of spending) and what you have today.
3. Whether you have term and health insurance.
4. A guess at your current savings rate.

Guess freely. The point of the next few chapters is to replace those guesses with measurements, and the gap between your guess and the truth is usually the most interesting thing you learn in your first month.
